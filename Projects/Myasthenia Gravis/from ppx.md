# Myasthenia Gravis Medication & Interaction Safety Manual (2026)

This manual is a RAG-ready reference on medications, supplements, and exposures that can worsen myasthenia gravis (MG). It combines consensus lists from MGFA and academic reviews with detailed mechanism notes so both humans and LLMs can use it safely.

## How to Use This Manual

This document is designed as both a human-readable safety guide and an AI-ready knowledge source. Each section:

- Uses clear headings and short summaries.
- Labels drugs with a **risk class** on a common caution scale.
- Includes mechanism notes so the reasoning is visible, not just the rule.
- Keeps original-style references (MGFA sheets, pharmacy reviews) so a future model can trace back to source material.

Always treat this manual as **education, not medical orders**. Final decisions must go through the treating neurologist, anesthesiologist, or other clinicians.

---

## Risk Caution Scale (Not All Or Nothing)

Not every medication here is a hard "never." To avoid black‑and‑white thinking, this manual uses a gradient:

- **Class 1 – Absolute Avoid**  
  Strong consensus that this drug can rapidly trigger crisis or de novo MG. Only consider if there is no alternative and ventilatory support is immediately available.

- **Class 2 – High Risk (Situational)**  
  Clearly associated with serious worsening. May be used only when there is no safer option and with close monitoring.

- **Class 3 – Caution / Use With Care**  
  Common drugs that *can* worsen MG but are often needed (statins, beta‑blockers, some psych meds). Use lowest effective dose, start slow, monitor.

- **Class 4 – Paradoxical / Phase Risk**  
  Drugs that are core MG therapies (like steroids) but have a known early worsening window.

- **Class 5 – Generally Safe With Notes**  
  Drugs considered safe in MG when used normally, with a few things to watch.

Every drug entry below is labeled with one of these classes so it is clear this is **a risk gradient, not a yes/no universe**.

## Why MG Is So Sensitive to Medications

MG destroys the neuromuscular safety margin. In healthy muscle, each nerve impulse releases far more acetylcholine (ACh) than needed and there are many spare receptors; MG strips this away, so even small pharmacologic hits can push the system into failure.

Drugs can worsen MG by:

- Blocking ACh release presynaptically.
- Blocking or desensitizing AChRs postsynaptically.
- Directly relaxing muscle or changing excitability.
- Triggering new autoimmune activity or flares.
- Depressing central respiratory drive on top of weak breathing muscles.

The rest of the manual follows this logic: **what the drug does**, plus **how big the risk really is**, plus **what current MG guidance says**.

## Class 1 – Absolute Avoid

These agents have strong evidence and MGFA‑style warnings that they can cause rapid, severe neuromuscular failure. In practice, they should be treated as "do not use" for MG unless literally no alternative exists and the patient is in a fully monitored setting.

### Ketolide Antibiotic – Telithromycin

**Risk class:** Class 1 – Absolute Avoid.

- **Why:** Potent postsynaptic AChR blockade with multiple reports of abrupt, life‑threatening respiratory failure in MG.
- **Guidance:** Listed by MGFA with a clear "should not be used" warning in MG; FDA carries a black box warning related to MG exacerbation.

### Fluoroquinolone Antibiotics

**Risk class:** Class 1 – Absolute Avoid (practically High Risk in emergencies).

- **Examples:** Ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, norfloxacin, ofloxacin.
- **Why:** Dual hit – presynaptic inhibition of ACh release and postsynaptic ion channel block. Crises can appear within minutes to a few days of starting therapy.
- **Guidance:** FDA black box warning for MG; MGFA says "use cautiously, if at all" but many centers treat them as functional contraindications.

### Botulinum Toxin (Cosmetic or Therapeutic)

**Risk class:** Class 1 – Absolute Avoid.

- **Why:** Irreversible SNARE cleavage at the presynaptic terminal, shutting down ACh release until new terminals grow.
- **Guidance:** MGFA and multiple reviews recommend avoiding botulinum toxin entirely in MG patients.

### D‑Penicillamine

**Risk class:** Class 1 – Absolute Avoid.

- **Why:** Strongly associated with inducing de novo autoimmune MG by triggering anti‑AChR antibody production.
- **Guidance:** Described in reviews as strongly associated with MG onset; avoid in anyone with known MG.

### Intravenous Magnesium Sulfate

**Risk class:** Class 1 – Absolute Avoid.

- **Why:** Powerful presynaptic calcium channel antagonist; IV doses can abruptly wipe out ACh release and precipitate crisis.
- **Guidance:** Cited as potentially dangerous and should be used only if absolutely necessary, with close monitoring; functionally treated as "avoid."

## Class 2 – High Risk (Situational but Dangerous)

These drugs have solid evidence of making MG much worse. They are sometimes unavoidable (e.g., in sepsis), but need neuromuscular and respiratory monitoring and explicit risk‑benefit discussion.

### Aminoglycoside Antibiotics

**Risk class:** Class 2 – High Risk.

- **Examples:** Gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin, neomycin.
- **Why:** Block presynaptic calcium channels and directly depress ACh release, with additional postsynaptic effects.
- **Guidance:** Repeatedly linked to postoperative respiratory depression and rapid worsening; use only when no alternatives cover the organism.

### Macrolide Antibiotics

**Risk class:** Class 2 – High Risk.

- **Examples:** Erythromycin, azithromycin, clarithromycin.
- **Why:** Impair quantal ACh release and can precipitate abrupt weakness; case reports show crises within minutes of dosing.
- **Guidance:** Often listed as "may worsen MG – use cautiously, if at all"; many neurologists try to avoid them entirely if other options exist.

### Antiarrhythmics & Antimalarials (Quinidine / Quinine / Chloroquine / Hydroxychloroquine)

**Risk class:** Class 2 – High Risk.

- **Why:** Quinidine and quinine have curare‑like postsynaptic effects; chloroquine/hydroxychloroquine can both worsen established MG and precipitate new MG.
- **Guidance:** Quinine is prohibited for leg cramps in MG; antimalarials for autoimmune disease require close monitoring for new bulbar or respiratory weakness.

### Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors (ICIs)

**Risk class:** Class 2 – High Risk (Oncology Determined).

- **Examples:** Pembrolizumab, nivolumab, atezolizumab, avelumab, durvalumab, ipilimumab.
- **Why:** Remove immune brakes, allowing explosive autoantibody production; can cause de novo MG or severe flares, sometimes with myositis and myocarditis.
- **Guidance:** Must be managed jointly by oncology and neurology as a high‑risk, high‑benefit decision.

### Central Respiratory Depressants at High Doses

**Risk class:** Class 2 – High Risk (perioperative / ICU).

- **Examples:** High‑dose opioids, benzodiazepines, propofol in patients with known respiratory weakness.
- **Why:** Central medullary depression layered on top of weak diaphragm and intercostals rapidly leads to hypoventilation and CO₂ retention.
- **Guidance:** Necessary for surgery and ICU care, but doses must be minimized and monitored in MG; avoid oversedation in unmonitored settings.

## Class 3 – Caution / Use With Care

These drugs are common and often needed for other serious conditions (cardiac, lipid, psychiatric, autoimmune). They are **not** banned, but they do carry reproducible risks and should be used at the lowest effective dose with careful monitoring.

### Beta‑Blockers

**Risk class:** Class 3 – Caution.

- **Examples:** Propranolol, metoprolol, atenolol, ophthalmic timolol.
- **Why:** Can depress neuromuscular transmission and unmask or worsen weakness in some patients.
- **Guidance:** Use only when clearly indicated and monitor for new ptosis, diplopia, or fatigue.

### Calcium Channel Blockers

**Risk class:** Class 3 – Caution.

- **Examples:** Verapamil, felodipine, nifedipine.
- **Why:** Isolated case reports link them to worsened weakness, possibly via interference with presynaptic calcium entry.
- **Guidance:** Not a formal contraindication, but many suggest starting low and watching closely for breathing or bulbar changes.

### Statins

**Risk class:** Class 3 – Caution.

- **Examples:** Atorvastatin, simvastatin, rosuvastatin, pravastatin.
- **Why:** Can worsen pre‑existing MG or trigger new MG; mechanisms include mitochondrial toxicity and immune shifts toward autoantibody production.
- **Guidance:** Use if cardiovascular benefits are strong, but at lowest dose and with tight MG symptom monitoring.

### Psychotropics (Lithium & Antipsychotics)

**Risk class:** Class 3 – Caution.

- **Examples:** Lithium, haloperidol, clozapine, phenothiazines, some atypicals.
- **Why:** Lithium both boosts T‑cell activity and reduces AChR expression; antipsychotics can depress neuromuscular transmission pre‑ and postsynaptically.
- **Guidance:** Use when psychiatric need is strong, but titrate carefully and monitor for worsening MG.

### Anticonvulsants

**Risk class:** Class 3 – Caution.

- **Examples:** Carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, gabapentin, ethosuximide (from review tables).
- **Why:** Several can impair neuromuscular transmission or cause sedation that worsens functional weakness.
- **Guidance:** If needed for seizures or neuropathic pain, neurologist should be involved; watch for new ptosis or fatigability after initiation.

### Oral Magnesium, Zinc, Vitamin D (High Doses)

**Risk class:** Class 3 – Caution.

- **Why:** High‑dose oral magnesium and zinc can replicate presynaptic blockade and interfere with immunosuppressant absorption; mega‑dose vitamin D may disturb calcium‑dependent ACh release.
- **Guidance:** Dietary‑level doses and standard correction of deficiency are generally safe; avoid laxative‑level magnesium, zinc above ~40 mg/day, and experimental mega‑dose vitamin D without specialist oversight.

### Cannabis (THC / CBD)

**Risk class:** Class 3–4 – High Caution / Unknown.

- **Why:** THC and CBD can cause sedation and central respiratory depression; CBD also strongly inhibits CYP3A4/2C19, disturbing levels of immunosuppressants and steroids.
- **Guidance:** No robust human MG trials; given respiratory risk and interaction potential, any use should be disclosed to neurology and used extremely cautiously, if at all.

## Class 4 – Paradoxical / Phase‑Dependent Risk

Some drugs are **core MG treatments** but have a short early phase where they can make weakness worse before they help.

### Corticosteroids (Prednisone, Dexamethasone, Methylprednisolone)

**Risk class:** Class 4 – Paradoxical Risk.

- **Why:** High‑dose initiation can cause a marked worsening in the first 1–10 days by depressing motor nerve terminal function and lowering ACh release before immune benefits manifest.
- **Guidance:** International guidelines recommend either low‑and‑slow titration or covering the start of high doses with IVIG or plasma exchange in higher‑risk patients.

### Acetylcholinesterase Inhibitors (Pyridostigmine)

**Risk class:** Class 4 – Dose‑Related Risk.

- **Why:** Essential symptomatic therapy, but overdosing can cause cholinergic crisis with increased secretions and respiratory compromise that can mimic or compound myasthenic crisis.
- **Guidance:** Dosing changes should be supervised; sudden weakness right after a dose may suggest excess rather than lack.

## Class 5 – Generally Safe With Notes

These drugs are usually safe in MG when used in standard ways.

### Beta‑Lactam and Related Antibiotics

- **Examples:** Penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, nitrofurantoin, metronidazole, vancomycin.
- **Guidance:** Reviews list these as rarely implicated in MG worsening; they are generally preferred over fluoroquinolones/macrolides when appropriate.

### Local Anesthetics

- **Examples:** Lidocaine, bupivacaine when used in local or regional blocks.
- **Guidance:** Local/regional use is usually safe; large IV doses or continuous infusions require caution but are rarely needed.

### Common Analgesics

- **Examples:** Acetaminophen, NSAIDs such as ibuprofen and naproxen.
- **Guidance:** Most guides list these as safe in MG; no specific neuromuscular risks beyond usual renal/GI concerns.

## Perioperative & ICU Pharmacovigilance

### Non‑Depolarizing Neuromuscular Blockers (NMBAs)

**Risk class:** Class 2 – High Risk (Extreme Sensitivity).

- **Examples:** Rocuronium, vecuronium, atracurium, cisatracurium, mivacurium.
- **Why:** MG patients have fewer AChRs and therefore need only a fraction of typical doses; standard doses can cause prolonged, hard‑to‑reverse paralysis.
- **Guidance:** Use minimal possible dose with neuromuscular monitoring or avoid if feasible (e.g., awake intubation).

### Depolarizing NMBAs – Succinylcholine

**Risk class:** Class 2–3 – Complex Risk.

- **Why:** MG patients are relatively resistant to standard doses but can develop prolonged Phase II blocks due to altered cholinesterase activity.
- **Guidance:** Many anesthesia guidelines prefer minimizing or avoiding succinylcholine and tailoring doses only with full awareness of MG status.

### Sedatives, Opioids, and Post‑Op Monitoring

- **Why:** Central respiratory depression plus weak respiratory muscles is a dangerous combination.
- **Guidance:** Use the lowest effective doses; extend post‑op monitoring and be generous with ICU admission when MG status is moderate–severe.

## OTC Agents, Supplements, and Lifestyle Exposures

### Magnesium (Oral)

- **Risk class:** Class 3 – Caution.
- **Guidance:** Avoid high‑dose laxatives and "sleep" formulations; normal dietary magnesium and modest repletion are usually safe.

### Zinc

- **Risk class:** Class 3 – Caution.
- **Guidance:** Avoid chronic high‑dose zinc; dietary intake is preferred. Watch for interactions with mycophenolate and azathioprine.

### Vitamin D

- **Risk class:** Class 3 – Caution at Mega‑Doses.
- **Guidance:** Correct deficiency with usual regimens; avoid experimental mega‑dose protocols without close supervision.

### Cannabis (THC/CBD)

- **Risk class:** Class 3–4 – High Caution / Unknown.
- **Guidance:** Avoid heavy recreational use; disclose any use to neurology due to interaction and respiratory risks.

### Alcohol

- **Risk class:** Class 3 – Caution.

- **Why:** Central nervous system depressant that can worsen fatigue, impair coordination, and, at high doses, depress respiration; also adds fall and aspiration risk.
- **Guidance:** Light use may be tolerated in some patients, but binge drinking or combining alcohol with sedatives, opioids, or benzodiazepines is unsafe in MG.

## Hormonal and Reproductive Considerations

### Hormonal Contraception & HRT

- **Risk class:** Class 3 – Caution / Individualized.

- **What we know:** MG can fluctuate with menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and postpartum; some reports suggest symptom changes with hormone shifts, but large controlled data are limited.
- **Guidance:** Oral contraceptives and hormone replacement therapy are not on standard "avoid" lists; decisions should balance MG status with clotting and pregnancy risks.

### Pregnancy‑Related Drugs

- Obstetric use of **IV magnesium sulfate** remains a key red flag (Class 1 absolute avoid in MG).
- Anesthesia, antibiotics, and ICU sedation choices should follow the same MG risk logic as above, with extra caution for fetal safety.

---

## Environmental, Chemical, and Biological Triggers (Brief)

- **Organophosphate insecticides** can cause cholinergic crisis‑like states and are dangerous to MG patients.
- **Severe infections** (viral or bacterial) often precipitate MG flares independent of the drugs used to treat them.
- **Heat and overexertion** can magnify fatigable weakness and interact with drug effects.
