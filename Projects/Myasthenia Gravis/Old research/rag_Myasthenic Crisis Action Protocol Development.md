Myasthenic Crisis Action Protocol Development
This document outlines a comprehensive emergency framework for identifying and managing a myasthenic crisis, the most severe manifestation of Myasthenia Gravis. It is designed for patients, caregivers, and emergency medical personnel to ensure rapid triage and avoid life-threatening pharmacological errors. This resource helps solve the problem of clinical misdiagnosis and improper respiratory monitoring in acute care settings.
1.0 Introduction and Epidemiological Context of Myasthenia Gravis
This section provides a clinical overview of Myasthenia Gravis, its global prevalence, and the specific physiological risks associated with the disorder. It establishes the volatile nature of the disease and the significant percentage of patients who will face a life-threatening crisis.

Acquired myasthenia gravis (MG) represents a chronic, highly heterogeneous, and historically unpredictable autoimmune disorder of neuromuscular transmission. The foundational pathology of the disease arises from the production and binding of pathogenic autoantibodies to critical structural components of the neuromuscular junction, most predominantly the acetylcholine receptor (AChR) situated on the postsynaptic membrane. In a smaller, yet clinically significant subset of the patient population, the immune assault targets the muscle-specific tyrosine kinase (MuSK) or the lipoprotein receptor-related protein 4 (LRP4), while others remain seronegative despite exhibiting identical clinical phenotypes. The global epidemiological burden is substantial, with the incidence of the disorder ranging from 0.3 to 2.8 per 100,000 individuals, ultimately affecting an estimated 700,000 people worldwide.

The defining clinical hallmark of myasthenia gravis is fluctuating, fatigable muscle weakness that typically worsens proportionally with physical exertion or environmental stressors and improves following periods of rest. The clinical presentation spans a vast spectrum of severity, ranging from localized ocular weakness—manifesting exclusively as ptosis and diplopia—to profound, generalized paralysis affecting the appendicular, bulbar, and respiratory musculature. While the advent and increasing utilization of targeted immunomodulating therapies—such as systemic corticosteroids, rituximab, eculizumab, and methotrexate—have drastically improved the long-term prognosis, life expectancy, and quality of life for patients over recent decades, the disorder remains inherently volatile.

Despite these pharmacological advancements, a subset of the patient population remains vulnerable to a rapid, life-threatening escalation of symptoms known as a myasthenic crisis. Epidemiological data indicates that a myasthenic crisis occurs in approximately 15% to 20% of patients diagnosed with MG at least once during their lifetime. This catastrophic event represents the most severe manifestation of the disease and requires immediate, highly specialized emergency medical intervention.

To standardize the global management of this complex disease and mitigate the high historical mortality rates associated with myasthenic crisis, the Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America (MGFA) appointed a dedicated Task Force in October 2013. This initiative convened an elite panel of 15 international experts in neuromuscular disease. Utilizing the rigorous RAND/UCLA appropriateness methodology—which requires multiple rounds of anonymous voting to achieve a strict 80% consensus threshold—the panel published the formal International Consensus Guidance for the Management of Myasthenia Gravis in 2016. In February 2019, the international panel was reconvened, expanding its geographical representation, to meticulously evaluate new clinical evidence. This resulted in the publication of the 2020 International Consensus Guidance Update, which provided refined, universally accepted recommendations on early immunosuppression, surgical thymectomy, the integration of novel biologics, and the critical management protocols for impending and manifest myasthenic crises.

This exhaustive report synthesizes these internationally recognized guidelines, integrating them with phenomenological patient experiences and rigorous clinical data. The objective is to construct a comprehensive, patient-facing emergency action protocol that bridges the critical communication gap between patients experiencing acute neuromuscular failure and the emergency medical responders tasked with their triage and resuscitation.
2.0 The Pathophysiology of Exacerbation Versus Manifest Crisis
This section clarifies the diagnostic boundaries between a standard symptom flare and a manifest myasthenic crisis. It defines the point at which mechanical respiratory failure necessitates clinical classification as a crisis.

Understanding the precise clinical trajectory of a myasthenic emergency requires a rigid diagnostic distinction between an MG exacerbation—commonly referred to within the patient community as a "flare"—and a manifest myasthenic crisis. The physiological mechanisms driving these two states are identical in origin but vastly different in their threat to immediate patient survival.

An MG flare delineates a period in which a patient experiences a significant, often debilitating increase in the frequency or severity of their baseline myasthenic symptoms. During an exacerbation, autoimmune-mediated weakness may spread unpredictably across various muscle groups. Patients routinely experience heightened diplopia (double vision), severe ptosis (drooping eyelids), profound dysarthria (slurred speech), dysphagia (difficulty swallowing), and pronounced appendicular weakness that results in an unsteady, stumbling gait or the complete inability to elevate the upper extremities. While an exacerbation drastically impairs a patient's functional independence and dramatically degrades their quality of life, it is diagnostically distinct because it does not involve the critical, mechanical failure of the respiratory muscles to the extent that artificial ventilatory support is required.

Conversely, a manifest myasthenic crisis—formally classified within the clinical literature as MGFA Class V—is strictly defined by respiratory compromise. In this advanced state of neuromuscular blockade, the muscles primarily responsible for respiration, specifically the diaphragm and the intercostal muscles, become too fatigued to facilitate adequate tidal volume. The patient simply cannot generate the mechanical force required to move sufficient air in and out of the lungs. Furthermore, respiratory failure in MG is almost universally compounded by severe bulbar dysfunction; the pharyngeal and laryngeal muscles that are responsible for keeping the upper airway open and clear of secretions become profoundly weak, vastly increasing the risk of mechanical airway collapse, choking, and aspiration pneumonia.

A crisis typically develops progressively over a period of several days or weeks of worsening symptoms, entering a precarious transitional phase clinically designated as an "impending crisis". However, in rare instances, particularly when exposed to chemical triggers, the transition from stability to crisis can precipitate within hours. The precipitating triggers for these events are highly variable across the patient population but most commonly include systemic infections (particularly respiratory tract infections like pneumonia or bronchitis), invasive surgical interventions (including therapeutic thymectomy), pregnancy and the physiological shifts of the postpartum period, extreme emotional or physical stress, excessive environmental heat, and the inadvertent administration of contraindicated pharmacological agents.
3.0 Early Warning Signs Preceding Respiratory Failure
This section details the anatomical precursors to respiratory collapse, including bulbar, cervical, and appendicular weakness. It explains how to identify the subtle shifts that indicate a patient is moving toward an impending crisis.

Recognizing the preliminary clinical signs of an impending crisis is the single most critical factor in preventing a fatal respiratory arrest. Because myasthenic crisis presents fundamentally differently from other conventional forms of respiratory failure—such as asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or pulmonary embolism—patients and their caregivers must be meticulously trained to identify subtle neuromuscular deficits long before total diaphragmatic collapse occurs. The early warning signs are anatomically categorized into bulbar weakness, cervical weakness, and restrictive respiratory distress.
3.1 Bulbar and Cervical Indicators of Decline
This subsection describes the specific cranial nerve and neck muscle failures that often precede respiratory arrest. It highlights symptoms such as "dropped head syndrome" and the inability to clear oral secretions.

Bulbar symptoms are frequently the most immediate precursors to respiratory failure, as they indicate a profound breakdown in the brainstem-innervated musculature. Patients must maintain extreme vigilance for severe dysphagia, which often begins as difficulty swallowing solid, fibrous foods and rapidly progresses to the inability to swallow medications, liquids, and eventually the patient's own saliva. This is frequently accompanied by nasal regurgitation, where liquids escape through the nasal cavity during attempts to swallow, indicating severe palatal weakness. As oral secretions pool in the posterior pharynx, patients will experience frequent choking and gagging, even while at rest. Simultaneously, patients often display progressive dysarthria; this manifests as a noticeable change in vocal quality, characterized by a nasal, hyper-nasal, or severely slurred voice that deteriorates further with prolonged speaking. Additionally, the loss of facial expression—often termed the "myasthenic snarl"—and severe difficulty chewing, which may cause the jaw to hang open passively, indicate critical cranial nerve exhaustion.

While generalized limb weakness is common in baseline MG, a sudden and profound exacerbation in the cervical and appendicular regions often accompanies the onset of respiratory decline. The presentation of "dropped head syndrome" is a severe warning sign; this occurs when the extensor muscles of the neck become so profoundly weak that the patient is entirely unable to hold their head upright without manual support from their own hands. This cervical failure is often mirrored by rapid appendicular failure, rendering the patient suddenly unable to stand, walk, or raise their arms, particularly when this generalized exhaustion occurs in tandem with the earliest sensations of shortness of breath.
3.2 Mechanical Respiratory Failure and Self-Quantification
This subsection explains the "bellows failure" nature of MG respiratory distress and provides instructions for the single breath count test. It outlines the visual signs of paradoxical breathing and accessory muscle use.

Unlike primary pulmonary diseases, respiratory distress in myasthenia gravis is a mechanical bellows failure. The lung tissue itself is healthy, but the muscular pump driving the inflation is failing. One of the most terrifying early signs is orthopnea, which is the absolute inability to breathe while lying flat. Patients frequently report an overwhelming sensation of suffocation or "air hunger" the moment they become supine, forcing them to sleep sitting upright in a chair or propped against multiple pillows. During conversations, a patient approaching crisis will suffer from speech interruption, forced to pause in the middle of a short sentence to gasp for a breath due to a severe lack of vital capacity.

As the mechanical failure deepens, the patient will exhibit a weak, ineffective cough that completely fails to clear accumulating mucus and pooled saliva from the throat, vastly increasing the immediate risk of aspiration. Visually, observers may note paradoxical breathing and accessory muscle use; this is characterized by the visible retraction or pulling-in of the muscles between the ribs, around the neck, and in the abdomen during inhalation as the body desperately attempts to recruit secondary muscles to assist the failing diaphragm.

To objectively quantify this decline at home, patients are taught the single breath count test. This is a highly effective, self-administered clinical tool. After taking a maximal inhalation, the patient attempts to count out loud at a normal, conversational pace. The inability to count past 20 on a single breath correlates strongly with a dangerously low forced vital capacity (FVC), signaling that the respiratory reserves have been exhausted. When an MG patient experiences resting shortness of breath coupled with any combination of severe bulbar, cervical, or paradoxical respiratory symptoms, the window of impending crisis has closed; they have entered a manifest crisis, and immediate emergency medical intervention is absolutely mandatory.

To effectively manage the inherently unpredictable nature of myasthenia gravis, patients must operate within a structured, highly disciplined, tiered emergency action plan. 5.0 Emergency Department Arrival and Communication Protocol
This section offers strategies for navigating the Emergency Department, focusing on overcoming common misdiagnoses and logistical hurdles. it provides a specific script for patients and advocates to use during triage.

The transition from the home environment to the Emergency Department (ED) represents a period of extreme vulnerability for the myasthenia gravis patient. Because MG is a rare orphan disease, emergency medical technicians (EMTs), triage nurses, and attending ED physicians often do not possess the specialized clinical experience required to instantly recognize the highly nuanced, atypical presentation of a myasthenic crisis. Consequently, patients frequently face systemic medical hurdles, including perilous misdiagnoses, medical gaslighting, or the inadvertent administration of contraindicated pharmacological treatments that can rapidly precipitate a fatal outcome.

Upon arrival, or the moment communication is established with paramedics, the patient or their designated advocate must deliver a concise, medically unassailable script designed to immediately orient the clinical team to the sheer severity of the physiological collapse. The communication must be authoritative and explicit:

"I have myasthenia gravis, confirmed by a blood test and diagnosed by a neurologist. I am currently experiencing a myasthenic crisis characterized by respiratory muscle failure and severe swallowing difficulty. My breathing is becoming rapidly compromised. Please contact my neurologist immediately. I have a specialized emergency medical protocol card that outlines my absolute contraindicated drugs and the highly specific respiratory assessments required for my condition."
5.1 Mitigating Bias: Intoxication and Psychiatric Misdiagnosis
This subsection addresses the "intoxication fallacy" and the risk of being mislabeled with Functional Neurological Disorder (FND). It emphasizes the value of a signed neurologist's letter to validate the physical diagnosis.

The phenomenological reality of navigating the ER with an active MG exacerbation is fraught with systemic cognitive biases that must be actively managed by the patient advocate upon arrival. The first of these is the intoxication fallacy. Due to the presence of severe dysarthria, marked ptosis, and an unsteady, stumbling gait, EMTs and ER triage nurses routinely misinterpret the physical presentation of an MG crisis as acute alcohol or drug intoxication. It is imperative that the emergency card and the vocal patient advocate explicitly dispel this assumption the moment triage begins, ensuring that valuable time is not wasted administering toxicology screens while the patient's respiratory muscles progressively fail.

Furthermore, in high-stress ER environments, if an attending physician observes a patient exhibiting severe, generalized weakness without an obvious traumatic, cardiovascular, or immediately apparent pulmonary etiology, they may incorrectly label the event as a Functional Neurological Disorder (FND) or a severe psychogenic panic attack. Within the myasthenia gravis community, having a historically confirmed diagnosis arbitrarily stripped and replaced with FND on an acute medical chart is recognized as an actively harmful, potentially lethal occurrence. Once the FND label is documented, it becomes exceedingly difficult for the patient's physical decline to be taken seriously by subsequent shifts, resulting in catastrophic delays in life-saving treatment. Providing a physically signed, clinical letterhead from the primary neurologist detailing the verified MG diagnosis is the absolute most effective shield against this specific psychological misdiagnosis.
5.2 Critical Respiratory Assessments and Secretion Management
This subsection warns against reliance on pulse oximetry and explains why FVC and NIF measurements are the only reliable metrics for MG. It also covers the use of BiPAP and Yankauer suction catheters.

Perhaps the most lethal clinical trap in the acute management of an MG crisis is an over-reliance on pulse oximetry. Breathing difficulty in MG is strictly related to the mechanical failure of the diaphragmatic bellows, not an inherent defect in the pulmonary tissue itself. Because the alveoli of the lungs remain completely healthy and capable of gas exchange, oxygen transfer into the bloodstream remains highly efficient until the very late stages of total respiratory collapse. Consequently, a patient in the terrifying throes of an impending crisis may display a falsely reassuring oxygen saturation (SpO2) of 98% to 100%, leading untrained staff to erroneously dismiss the severity of the respiratory distress and deny intubation.

Patients and advocates must explicitly instruct the clinical staff that pulse oximetry is never a reliable measurement of respiratory status in a myasthenic patient. Instead, the absolute standard of care dictates that the medical team must immediately measure the patient's Forced Vital Capacity (FVC) and Negative Inspiratory Force (NIF) utilizing bedside spirometry. These vital metrics objectively assess the actual mechanical strength of the respiratory muscles. A baseline must be established immediately upon arrival and trended consistently—typically every few hours, or more frequently if the patient's work of breathing increases—to objectively track the decline in ventilatory power.

Secretion management is another critical priority upon admission. Patients suffering from severe bulbar weakness are at an extreme risk of choking on their own accumulating oral secretions or aspirating them into the lungs. The patient advocate must immediately request access to a Yankauer suction catheter to clear pooled saliva from the patient's mouth. If the patient is actively choking and an airway obstruction is suspected, standard basic life support, including the Heimlich maneuver, should be utilized; otherwise, maintaining an open airway in an upright, seated position is paramount.

Should the FVC and NIF levels drop to critical, predetermined thresholds, artificial ventilatory support must be initiated. BiPAP (noninvasive positive pressure ventilation) is heavily preferred as the initial alternative to endotracheal intubation, provided the patient does not have hypercapnia and importantly, retains enough bulbar strength to clear their own secretions without aspirating into the tight-fitting mask. If BiPAP proves insufficient to maintain tidal volume, or if the bulbar muscles completely collapse, endotracheal intubation and admission to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) are strictly required.
6.0 Contraindicated and Cautionary Pharmacological Agents
This section provides a detailed pharmacological risk profile for MG patients. It highlights specific antibiotic, mineral, and sedative classes that can cause rapid neuromuscular blockade.

In the acute, chaotic setting of the Emergency Department, the administration of standard therapeutic drugs—such as broad-spectrum antibiotics, sedatives, or cardiovascular agents—can act as devastating chemical triggers. These agents can cause a rapid, medically induced blockade of the neuromuscular junction, plunging a previously stable MG patient directly into a manifest crisis within minutes or hours of administration. The pathophysiology of myasthenia gravis results in severely diminished reserves at the neuromuscular junction; therefore, even a drug that causes a minute, sub-clinical interference in nerve-to-muscle transmission in a healthy individual can cause catastrophic, generalized paralysis in an MG patient.

7.0 Acute Clinical Interventions: Clinical Efficacy and Patient Phenomenology
This section evaluates the two primary crisis management therapies: IVIg and PLEX. It balances clinical data on efficacy with a focus on the lived experience and sensory realities of the patient during treatment.

When an impending or manifest myasthenic crisis occurs, or when patients present with significant respiratory and bulbar dysfunction that threatens their airway, immediate intervention with short-term, rapid-acting immunomodulating therapies is the absolute standard of care. The 2020 International Consensus Guidance Update heavily reaffirms that Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIg) and Therapeutic Plasma Exchange (PLEX, also known as plasmapheresis) are the definitive mainstays of crisis management.

Both therapies serve to rapidly reduce the autoimmune attack on the neuromuscular junction, halting the progression of respiratory failure and beginning the process of restoring muscular strength. While multiple clinical trials suggest that IVIg and PLEX possess roughly equal long-term efficacy in the treatment of myasthenic crisis, expert consensus heavily indicates that PLEX is often more profoundly effective and initiates clinical improvement far more rapidly. However, the selection between these two modalities is rarely simple; it hinges heavily on a complex matrix of individual patient characteristics, institutional experience and equipment availability, the viability of venous access, the patient's baseline hemodynamic stability, and the overall tolerability of severe side effects.
7.1 The Patient Phenomenology and Clinical Mechanics of IVIg
This subsection describes the process of IVIg therapy, from its biological source to the "grueling endurance" of multi-hour infusions. It covers pre-medication protocols and the risk of aseptic meningitis.

Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIg) is a concentrated, sterile solution of normal antibodies (immunoglobulins) that have been separated and pooled from the donated plasma of thousands of healthy individuals. By flooding the myasthenic patient's bloodstream with an overwhelming volume of healthy immunoglobulins, the therapy effectively downregulates the hyperactive immune system, neutralizing or displacing the pathogenic autoantibodies (such as AChR and MuSK) that are actively destroying the neuromuscular junction.

From the patient's perspective, IVIg is frequently favored over PLEX due to its significantly less invasive nature. The infusion only requires standard peripheral venous access—a standard IV line in the arm—rather than the surgical placement of a central line. The infusion itself utilizes a clear, colorless, or pale yellow liquid and is typically administered in a hospital bed or a comfortable outpatient infusion suite designed to reduce anxiety. According to strict clinical protocols, the dosage is calculated directly according to the patient's body weight, and the treatment course in a crisis scenario typically spans two to five consecutive days.

However, the infusion is a grueling endurance event. Each daily session lasts between two to six hours, as the massive volume of concentrated proteins must be administered very slowly to prevent shock; the infusion rate is gradually titrated upward to a maintenance level provided the patient exhibits no immediate adverse reactions. The physiological toll of introducing such a vast quantity of foreign proteins is immense. The process of preparing for an infusion is crucial; patients are heavily advised by their care teams to hyper-hydrate with copious fluids and electrolytes in the days leading up to the treatment to mitigate the most common and debilitating side effect: the excruciating post-IVIg headache.

To further suppress the body's natural rejection of the infusion, nurses routinely administer a heavy regimen of pre-medications immediately prior to the drip, typically including antihistamines (such as Benadryl), antipyretics (Tylenol), and NSAIDs (Advil) or systemic steroids. During the infusion, patients are subjected to intense, continuous monitoring. Vital signs—including blood pressure, pulse, and temperature—are assessed every 15 to 30 minutes to catch early signs of hypersensitivity or anaphylaxis. Common, immediate side effects that patients endure during the drip include a profound, heavy sense of fatigue, unexpected flushing, deep chills, persistent nausea, and flu-like muscle or joint pain. More severe, albeit rarer, complications include aseptic meningitis, allergic reactions, and an increased risk of thrombotic events.
7.2 The Patient Phenomenology and Clinical Mechanics of PLEX
This subsection explains the extracorporeal mechanics of PLEX and the necessity of central venous catheters. it describes the sensory shifts and the dramatic "up feeling" experienced upon symptom resolution.

Therapeutic Plasma Exchange (PLEX) is an advanced extracorporeal blood purification technique. It functions mechanically similarly to renal dialysis, though targeting the plasma rather than urea and metabolic waste. During the procedure, the patient's blood is continuously routed out of their body and pumped through a complex apheresis machine. The machine utilizes rapid centrifugation or specialized filtration to physically separate the liquid plasma—which contains the pathogenic autoantibodies actively blocking nerve transmission—from the red and white blood cells. The antibody-laden plasma is permanently discarded, and the patient's blood cells are mixed with a sterile substitute fluid and reinfused.

Clinical data underscores the incredibly rapid efficacy of PLEX in a crisis setting. Studies document that PLEX can yield an average 46% improvement in severity scores, clearing up to 71% of antibodies in days. However, PLEX is an aggressive, physically demanding procedure. achieveing adequate venous access is the most significant hurdle; it requires the surgical placement of a large-bore central venous catheter deeply into a major vein in the neck, chest, or groin. Patients frequently recount the repeated trauma and deep bruising of failed vein punctures.

During the sessions, patients are tethered to the machine and must remain still. Hemodynamic shifts are common, leading to sudden hypotension and terrifying sensations of lightheadedness. Furthermore, the citrate anticoagulant used in the circuit can cause hypocalcemia, manifesting as a metallic taste, circumoral paresthesia (tingling around the lips), and involuntary muscle twitching. Despite these unnatural sensations, the clinical turnaround is often described as miraculous, with patients reporting a sudden "up feeling" where drooping eyelids lift and the terrifying weight of respiratory failure disappears.

8.0 Constructing a Personalized MG Emergency Action Card
This section emphasizes the necessity of a portable medical proxy. It outlines the specific bolded warnings and clinical directives that must be included to ensure patient safety in the absence of a neurologist.

The ultimate cornerstone of out-of-hospital crisis preparedness is the meticulous creation, maintenance, and distribution of a personalized MG Emergency Action Card. Because myasthenia gravis triggers a unique physiological cascade that directly contradicts standard emergency medical protocols (such as the routine reliance on pulse oximetry or the liberal use of paralytics for rapid sequence intubation), the patient cannot rely on the inherent knowledge of local first responders. The emergency card serves as an authoritative, portable medical proxy that unequivocally dictates care when the patient is suffocating and entirely unable to speak.

The Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America (MGFA) strongly advocates that every diagnosed patient carry an updated, digitally fillable or printed emergency alert card in their physical wallet at all times. A meticulously constructed emergency packet should contain the following prioritized, highly specific elements:

Neurologist Letterhead: A formal, printed diagnosis signed by the primary neurologist. This establishes immediate credibility and neutralizes the risk of a psychiatric or intoxication misdiagnosis.
Core Statement: Prominent text stating: "I have myasthenia gravis (MG). Symptoms include weakness, difficulty speaking, and difficulty breathing/swallowing. These may be mistaken for intoxication."
Emergency Contacts: Names and 24/7 phone numbers for at least two caregivers who are intimately familiar with the patient's baseline.
Actionable Clinical Directives: Bold instructions stating: "Pulse oximetry is not a reliable measurement. Measure FVC and NIF immediately. Use Yankauer to suction secretions. Do not let patient lie flat."
Cautionary Drugs List: An exhaustive list of current medications and the "Black Box" contraindicated drugs that must never be administered.

Finally, supplementing the physical card with a durable medical alert bracelet stating "Myasthenia Gravis" ensures that even if the card is misplaced, the life-saving protocols remain accessible to the clinical team.
