1.0 Introduction and Pathophysiological Context
This section defines Myasthenia Gravis and explains the biological mechanisms behind neuromuscular junction disruption. It covers the roles of various autoantibodies and the shift from broad immunosuppression to targeted therapies.

Myasthenia gravis (MG) represents a paradigmatic, autoantibody-mediated autoimmune disorder characterized by fluctuating, fatigable weakness of the skeletal musculature. The fundamental pathology arises from disrupted signal transduction at the neuromuscular junction (NMJ), where the communication between motor nerve terminals and muscle fibers is biochemically interrupted. In approximately 85% of generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG) populations, the disease is driven by immunoglobulin G (IgG) autoantibodies targeting the postsynaptic nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (AChR). These pathogenic anti-AChR antibodies, predominantly of the IgG1 and IgG3 subclasses, impair neuromuscular transmission through a combination of mechanisms.

Most notably, they trigger the classical complement cascade, which culminates in the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC). The MAC physically degrades and simplifies the postsynaptic membrane, reducing the surface area available for neurotransmitter binding and resulting in profound functional weakness.

A smaller, yet clinically significant, subset of patients harbor autoantibodies directed against the muscle-specific tyrosine kinase (MuSK) or lipoprotein receptor-related protein 4 (LRP4). Unlike anti-AChR antibodies, anti-MuSK antibodies are typically of the IgG4 subclass. Because IgG4 antibodies do not strongly activate the complement cascade, their pathological mechanism differs; they directly inhibit the clustering of acetylcholine receptors required for efficient muscle contraction. A final cohort of patients exhibits seronegative myasthenia gravis, lacking detectable levels of any of these classical autoantibodies via standard assays, which historically complicated both definitive diagnosis and the selection of targeted therapeutic interventions.

For decades, the pharmacological management of MG remained relatively static, relying on a generalized, non-specific suppression of the immune system. However, the last decade—and specifically the period between 2023 and the current 2026 baseline—has witnessed a transformative acceleration in targeted therapeutic development. The clinical landscape has unequivocally shifted from the empirical, off-label use of broad-spectrum immunosuppressants to the precise deployment of monoclonal antibodies, macrocyclic peptides, and highly advanced cellular immunotherapies engineered to intercept specific vectors of the disease's immunopathology. This paradigm shift is actively redefining neurological treatment algorithms, focusing not merely on symptom mitigation, but on achieving rapid onset of action, sustained clinical remission, robust neuromuscular protection, and the absolute minimization of systemic therapeutic toxicity.

This comprehensive report rigorously examines the 2026 baseline standard of care, evaluates recently approved biological agents, dissects late-stage clinical trial data for emerging therapeutics, and synthesizes the broader implications of targeted immunotherapies on clinical practice, comparative effectiveness, and patient quality of life.
1.1 Symptomatic Management and Broad-Spectrum Immunosuppression
This subsection details the foundational treatments for MG, including acetylcholinesterase inhibitors and traditional immunosuppressants. It outlines the limitations of these therapies, such as long latency periods and systemic side effects.

The absolute first line of symptomatic defense relies on acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitors, such as pyridostigmine bromide, which is commercially available under trade names including Mestinon and Regonol. By inhibiting the enzymatic breakdown of acetylcholine in the synaptic cleft, pyridostigmine artificially increases the availability and dwell time of the neurotransmitter, thereby facilitating improved nerve-to-muscle signal transmission. While effective for mild symptomatic relief and widely utilized as background therapy, AChE inhibitors do not alter the underlying autoimmune disease process.

Consequently, they are frequently insufficient as monotherapy for generalized or severe disease presentations. Therefore, conventional disease-modifying therapy has historically depended on corticosteroids (such as prednisone) and non-steroidal broad-spectrum immunosuppressants, including:

Azathioprine
Mycophenolate mofetil
Calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus or cyclosporine)

While these conventional therapies can induce meaningful clinical improvement and even remission in a subset of patients, their pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles present severe, well-documented limitations. The most critical deficiency of this drug class is the prolonged latency period between therapy initiation and clinical response. Depending on the selected agent, the onset of observable clinical improvement ranges from 5 to 8 months, while maximum therapeutic efficacy may require 18 to 36 months of continuous administration.

Furthermore, the mechanism of action of these traditional agents relies on the indiscriminate suppression of the entire immune system. This lack of specificity results in a broad and often severe adverse event profile. Chronic corticosteroid use is intrinsically linked to deleterious metabolic, skeletal, and psychiatric side effects, severely impairing the patient's long-term health and overall quality of life. Real-world data indicates that traditional therapies fail to adequately control disease activity in up to 15% of patients, categorizing them as treatment-refractory and necessitating escalation to more aggressive modalities. In acute exacerbations or myasthenic crises, rapid interventions such as Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG) and plasma exchange (PLEX) are utilized; however, these require frequent, inconvenient, and resource-intensive pulsing.
1.2 The 2025 Association of British Neurologists (ABN) Guidelines
This subsection summarizes recent updates to clinical management frameworks for autoimmune myasthenia gravis. Key changes include standardized corticosteroid dosing, the role of thymectomy, and the early use of B-cell depletion and targeted biologics.

In response to the rapidly evolving evidentiary landscape and the introduction of novel biologics, major neurological societies have been forced to continually update their clinical management frameworks. The 2025 update of the Association of British Neurologists (ABN) guidelines for the management of autoimmune myasthenia gravis highlights several critical departures from historical practices established in their previous 2015 iteration.

The 2025 guidelines establish that the prescription of a daily corticosteroid regimen is now the recognized standard of practice, officially replacing the older, less effective alternate-day dosing regimens.
Integrating findings from landmark randomized controlled trials—most notably the international MGTX trial—the guidelines place a clear and definitive emphasis on the therapeutic benefits of early thymectomy in nonthymomatous MG, demonstrating that surgical intervention can effectively alter the disease course and reduce the required long-term dose of prednisone.
The 2025 ABN guidelines formally advocate for the early utilization of B-cell depletion therapy via rituximab. The updated recommendation advises initiating rituximab within the first year of generalized disease onset, an approach backed by recent randomized controlled trial evidence. However, the guidelines note that its utility in established, treatment-refractory MG is supported by less robust data, though still considered likely to be beneficial.
The guidelines formally recognize the ascending role of highly targeted biological agents—specifically those inhibiting the complement cascade and the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) pathways. These advanced therapeutics are now positioned as critical, evidence-based interventions for patients who exhibit suboptimal responses or severe intolerances to conventional broad-spectrum immunosuppression.
2.0 Targeting the Autoantibody: Neonatal Fc Receptor (FcRn) Inhibitors
This section explains the mechanism of FcRn inhibitors in reducing circulating IgG levels. It compares this mechanism to plasma exchange and introduces specific agents used in clinical practice.

A major biomedical breakthrough in the treatment of IgG-mediated autoimmune diseases over the past several years has been the successful commercialization of neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) inhibitors. Physiologically, the FcRn acts as a vital recycling mechanism for endogenous IgG. It binds to IgG antibodies that have been internalized by endothelial cells, preventing their lysosomal degradation and redirecting them back into the bloodstream, thereby extending their circulatory half-life.

FcRn inhibitors function by competitively occupying this receptor, thereby blocking the IgG recycling pathway. Deprived of the protective FcRn interaction, endogenous immunoglobulins—including the pathogenic autoantibodies responsible for MG—are directed toward rapid lysosomal destruction. This mechanism effectively achieves a 60% to 70% reduction in total circulating IgG levels, a clearance rate that is directly comparable to the physical removal of antibodies achieved by therapeutic plasma exchange (PLEX), but critically without the invasive, high-risk, and resource-intensive logistical requirements of extracorporeal filtration.
2.1 Efgartigimod and Rozanolixizumab
This subsection covers the clinical development and administration methods of efgartigimod and rozanolixizumab. It highlights the move toward patient autonomy with subcutaneous self-administration options.

Efgartigimod was the pioneering agent in this highly successful class, initially approved as an intravenous formulation under the trade name Vyvgart, and subsequently approved as a subcutaneous co-formulation with recombinant human hyaluronidase, marketed as Vyvgart Hytrulo. Administered in cyclical treatment courses consisting of four weekly doses, efgartigimod has demonstrated robust efficacy in anti-AChR positive populations, primarily validated through the extensive phase 3 ADAPT clinical trials. Further investigations, such as the actively recruiting ADAPT-EARLY trial, are currently evaluating the clinical outcomes of subcutaneous efgartigimod specifically in adults with new-onset generalized myasthenia gravis.

Rozanolixizumab (Rystiggo), a humanized IgG4 monoclonal antibody targeting the FcRn, received European Union approval in early 2025 following compelling data from the phase 3 MycarinG and MG0020 trials. Unlike efgartigimod, rozanolixizumab holds broad indications for both AChR-positive and MuSK-positive patient populations. A critical advancement introduced by rozanolixizumab is its specific optimization for patient self-administration. The phase 3, open-label, randomized, crossover MG0020 study explicitly evaluated the feasibility, efficacy, and safety of subcutaneous self-administration using either a programmable syringe pump or a manual push syringe. The trial, which included an initial training period followed by self-administration phases, reported highly successful administration metrics alongside robust clinical efficacy. Patients receiving the 10 mg/kg dosage achieved a 6.7-point reduction in the Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis (QMG) score by day 43, while those in the 7 mg/kg group achieved a 5.4-point reduction, both demonstrating statistically significant improvements over placebo. Patient-reported outcomes indicated a strong preference for the manual push method, highlighting a massive leap forward in patient autonomy.
2.2 Nipocalimab: Broad Spectrum Efficacy and FDA Approval
This subsection details the profile of nipocalimab, the most recent FcRn inhibitor to receive FDA approval. It describes its continuous dosing model and broad population coverage, including pediatric patients.

The most recent and arguably most versatile addition to the FcRn inhibitor class is nipocalimab (Imaavy), a fully human FcRn-blocking monoclonal antibody which received priority review and subsequent approval by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) on April 30, 2025. Administered via an initial 30-minute intravenous loading infusion followed by 15-minute maintenance infusions every two weeks, nipocalimab represents a departure from the purely cyclical dosing models of earlier FcRn inhibitors, offering a continuous, sustained suppression of IgG.

The regulatory approval of nipocalimab was heavily supported by the pivotal phase 3 Vivacity-MG3 clinical trial. The trial design was uniquely broad and highly inclusive, encompassing adult patients possessing antibodies against AChR, MuSK, or LRP4 who had previously demonstrated an inadequate response to standard-of-care (SOC) therapy. Across a 24-week double-blind period involving 153 antibody-positive patients, those receiving nipocalimab plus SOC exhibited a superior, statistically significant reduction in the Myasthenia Gravis Activities of Daily Living (MG-ADL) total score compared to placebo, achieving a least-squares mean change of –4.70 points versus –3.25 points for the control group. Correspondingly, the clinician-administered QMG score improved by an impressive –4.86 points in the treatment arm compared to a –2.05 point reduction in the placebo cohort.

Long-term safety and sustained disease control data derived from the ongoing open-label extension (OLE) of the Vivacity-MG3 study further solidify nipocalimab's clinical utility. With up to 128 weeks and 180 patient-years of follow-up presented extensively at the 2025 and 2026 American Academy of Neurology (AAN) annual meetings, the data confirms that treated patients maintain significant improvements in both MG-ADL and QMG scores over 84 weeks, operating concurrently with sustained reductions (up to 75%) in total circulating IgG. Crucially, nipocalimab selectively lowers harmful IgG levels without inducing widespread, generalized immunosuppression. Its approval extends to the broadest population of gMG patients to date, covering both adult and pediatric individuals aged 12 years and older.
2.3 Batoclimab: Late-Stage Pipeline Developments
This subsection provides an update on batoclimab, currently in late-stage clinical evaluation. It discusses the drug's potential across multiple autoimmune indications and anticipated regulatory milestones.

The FcRn inhibitor pipeline remains highly active into 2026, with batoclimab currently in late-stage clinical evaluation. As a fully human monoclonal antibody designed for subcutaneous administration, batoclimab's clinical program seeks to leverage deep IgG suppression to drive therapeutic outcomes across multiple indications. While specific phase 3 MG data remains under active review into April 2026, the drug has shown durable responses in related autoimmune conditions, such as uncontrolled Graves' disease and thyroid eye disease, where it demonstrated significant proptosis improvement corresponding with deep IgG suppression. Batoclimab represents a continued, heavy investment by pharmaceutical entities in refining and optimizing the FcRn blockade mechanism for diverse autoimmune indications, with further regulatory milestones anticipated throughout 2026.

3.0 Terminal Complement Pathway Inhibition: Protecting the Neuromuscular Junction
This section explains how complement inhibitors protect the postsynaptic membrane by halting the assembly of the membrane attack complex. It contrasts this downstream approach with the upstream antibody clearance of FcRn inhibitors.

While FcRn inhibitors focus upstream on accelerating the clearance of the pathogenic autoantibody from circulation, complement inhibitors act downstream directly at the site of pathology. In AChR-antibody positive MG, the binding of the autoantibody to the postsynaptic receptor triggers the classical complement cascade. This cascade leads to the enzymatic cleavage of the C5 protein into C5a and C5b, which is the final committed step required for the assembly of the membrane attack complex (MAC). The MAC subsequently initiates focal simplification, massive fluid influx, and the physical lysis of the muscle cell membrane. By pharmacologically binding to C5 and preventing its cleavage, complement inhibitors effectively halt the destructive downstream effects of the autoantibody, conferring immediate protection to the NMJ without directly altering circulating immunoglobulin levels.
3.1 First-Generation and Next-Generation Monoclonal Antibodies
This subsection discusses the evolution from eculizumab to ravulizumab. It emphasizes the improvements in dosing convenience and half-life extension in next-generation treatments.

Eculizumab, a humanized IgG2/4 monoclonal antibody, was the pioneering first-in-class C5 inhibitor approved for refractory AChR-positive gMG, following the highly successful phase 3 REGAIN trial. While highly effective at inducing rapid clinical improvement, eculizumab is limited by its requirement for frequent, cumbersome intravenous infusions, placing a significant logistical burden on both the patient and the healthcare infrastructure.

This limitation prompted the rapid development of ravulizumab (Ultomiris), an engineered iteration of eculizumab featuring distinct structural modifications that vastly extend its half-life. This extended half-life allows for highly convenient dosing intervals of up to eight weeks. Data from the CHAMPION-MG phase 3 trial corroborated its efficacy, confirming that sustained complement blockade translates to stable, long-term neuromuscular protection with a fraction of the infusion burden. Additional data presented at the 2026 AAN meeting continues to highlight real-world evidence and long-term safety of ravulizumab across neuroimmunological indications.
3.2 Peptide Macrocycles and Dual-Binding Nanobodies
This subsection covers innovative complement inhibitors like zilucoplan and the investigational nanobody gefurulimab. It highlights the trend toward small-molecule delivery and subcutaneous self-administration.

The intense drive toward patient autonomy and localized subcutaneous delivery has fueled the development of a newer, highly innovative generation of complement inhibitors. Zilucoplan (Zilbrysq) is a distinctly unique pharmacological entity within this class; rather than a large, complex monoclonal antibody, it is a short, 3.5 kDa macrocyclic peptide composed of 15 amino acids (including four unnatural variants) that specifically and potently inhibits C5/C5b. Approved by the FDA and EMA for AChR-positive gMG, zilucoplan is administered as a 0.3 mg/kg daily subcutaneous injection. Results from the phase 3 RAISE trial demonstrated a clinically meaningful and statistically significant placebo-corrected mean improvement of 2.12 points on the MG-ADL scale by week 12, with notable, measurable symptom improvements observed as early as the first week of therapy. A favorable safety profile was observed, with injection site bruising and headache being the most common treatment-emergent adverse events.

Looking toward the immediate future, the investigational agent gefurulimab (ALXN1720) represents a highly innovative approach to terminal complement inhibition. Gefurulimab is a bispecific, miniaturized, humanized nanobody engineered to bind both the C5 protein and human albumin. The interaction with human albumin significantly extends the drug's circulatory half-life, enabling a once-weekly subcutaneous self-administration protocol using a prefilled syringe or autoinjector.

New data from the global PREVAIL phase 3 clinical trial, heavily featured at the April 2026 American Academy of Neurology (AAN) Annual Meeting in Chicago, confirms gefurulimab's strong clinical profile. The trial, which randomized 254 AChR-positive adults to either weekly subcutaneous gefurulimab or placebo, reported a statistically significant and clinically meaningful reduction in the Myasthenia Gravis Composite (MGC) score, achieving a treatment difference of –3.1 points (P<0.0001) at Week 26. Secondary endpoints, including MG-ADL and QMG scores, also showed significant improvements. The rapid and sustained improvements demonstrated in the PREVAIL study provide robust clinical evidence supporting the anticipated regulatory submission of this self-administered C5 inhibitor, which is expected to further diversify patient treatment options in late 2026 and beyond.

Furthermore, the complement inhibitor pipeline continues to expand with novel mechanisms. Iptacopan, an oral complement factor B inhibitor, is currently being evaluated in a massive global Phase 3 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial. Enrolling 146 participants, the study aims to assess the efficacy and safety of a twice-daily hard gelatin capsule over a 6-month primary treatment period, highlighting a potential shift toward purely oral targeted biologics.
4.0 B-Cell Depletion Strategies: From CD20 to Precision CD19 Targeting
This section discusses the transition from non-specific CD20 depletion to targeted CD19 therapies. It explains why CD19 is a more effective target for eliminating autoantibody-producing plasmablasts.

B-cell depletion represents a foundational immunological strategy in the treatment of autoimmune disorders, aiming to completely eradicate the cellular source of autoantibody production rather than simply managing the secreted proteins. Historically, the off-label administration of rituximab, a chimeric monoclonal antibody targeting the CD20 surface antigen, has been widely employed for severe, refractory MG. While the 2025 ABN guidelines support early rituximab use within the first year of diagnosis, prospective clinical data has been unexpectedly ambiguous.

The phase 2 BeatMG (B Cell Targeted Treatment in Myasthenia Gravis) randomized controlled trial investigating rituximab in AChR-positive gMG failed to demonstrate a significant steroid-sparing effect compared to placebo. Although the safety profile was highly favorable, the trial's outcomes were potentially confounded by a high placebo response rate, low baseline B-cell counts resulting from prior immunosuppressive treatments, and a generally mild level of baseline disease severity among the enrolled cohort.

Beyond trial design, a critical biological limitation of CD20-targeted therapies is their inherent inability to eliminate plasmablasts and terminally differentiated plasma cells. These specific cell populations frequently downregulate the CD20 antigen as they mature, rendering them invisible to rituximab, yet they remain the primary, most potent factories for pathogenic autoantibody synthesis. To address this fundamental biological gap, therapeutic development has decisively shifted toward targeting the CD19 antigen. CD19 is expressed across a significantly broader continuum of the B-cell lineage, crucially encompassing the autoantibody-producing plasmablasts and early plasma cells.
4.1 Inebilizumab: Redefining Durable Efficacy and Steroid Sparing
This subsection details the FDA approval and clinical impact of inebilizumab. It highlights the drug's success in achieving significant steroid-sparing effects and durable symptom control with infrequent dosing.

The apex of this CD19-targeting strategy is inebilizumab (Uplizna), a humanized IgG1 anti-CD19 monoclonal antibody initially approved for neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder. Following a highly successful clinical development program in neuromuscular disease, the FDA granted approval for inebilizumab on December 11, 2025, for the treatment of gMG in adults who are either AChR-positive or MuSK-positive.

The FDA approval was anchored by the Myasthenia Gravis Inebilizumab Trial (MINT), the largest phase 3 biologic study to explicitly include both AChR-positive and MuSK-positive patient cohorts. At the 26-week primary endpoint for the combined population, inebilizumab induced a statistically significant improvement in the MG-ADL score (–4.2 points) compared to placebo (–2.2 points), alongside a superior reduction in the QMG score (–4.8 versus –2.3 points).

The MINT trial was uniquely groundbreaking in its architectural design; it was the first advanced biological trial in MG to successfully incorporate a strict, protocol-mandated steroid taper. Patients entering the trial on baseline corticosteroid therapy began a structured tapering protocol at Week 4, aggressively driving the dosage down to a mere 5 mg of prednisone per day by week 24. The ability of inebilizumab to maintain profound clinical efficacy and stabilize symptoms while actively withdrawing concomitant corticosteroids unequivocally validates its potent steroid-sparing capabilities. This offers a direct, evidence-based solution to one of the most pressing safety and quality-of-life concerns in long-term MG management.

Furthermore, late-breaking data presented at the April 2025 and 2026 AAN meetings revealed the extended 52-week data for the AChR-positive subpopulation. After an initial loading regimen consisting of two infusions, inebilizumab requires maintenance dosing only twice per year. Even on this highly infrequent administration schedule, the therapeutic effect proved deeply durable. At 52 weeks, 72.3% of patients in the inebilizumab arm exhibited a clinically meaningful improvement of >= 3 points on the MG-ADL scale, compared to only 45.2% in the placebo group. By directly targeting the upstream immunopathogenic mechanism and destroying the factories of antibody production, inebilizumab provides deep, durable symptom control with minimal ongoing treatment burden.

Other B-cell modulating therapies are also actively progressing through the pipeline:

Telitacicept: A fusion protein under investigation for B-cell modulation.
Povetacicept: Currently being evaluated for its ability to modulate B-cell activity.
Belimumab: A BAFF inhibitor that has shown promise in network meta-analyses for MG-ADL improvement.
5.0 The Cellular Immunotherapy Revolution: CAR-T Cells in Myasthenia Gravis
This section introduces Chimeric Antigen Receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy as the absolute vanguard of MG research. It explains the potential for an enduring "immune reset" and drug-free disease remission.

While monoclonal antibodies temporarily suppress or alter the immune response requiring chronic administration, the absolute vanguard of 2026 neuromuscular research is concentrated on cellular immunotherapies—specifically, Chimeric Antigen Receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy. Originally developed to eradicate refractory hematological malignancies, CAR-T therapy involves harvesting a patient's autologous T-cells, genetically engineering them ex vivo to express a receptor that binds to specific B-cell antigens, and infusing them back into the patient. These engineered "living drugs" actively hunt and destroy the precise B-cell populations responsible for autoimmune dysregulation, offering the unprecedented potential for an enduring "immune reset" and long-term, drug-free disease remission.
5.1 Descartes-08 (Rese-cel): Outpatient mRNA CAR-T Therapy
This subsection describes the unique mRNA-based approach of Descartes-08. It highlights the safety benefits of transient engineering, which eliminates the need for toxic preconditioning chemotherapy.

Descartes-08, also known under the investigational moniker rese-cel, represents a highly specialized adaptation of CAR-T technology engineered specifically for autoimmune disease by Cartesian Therapeutics. Traditional oncological CAR-T therapies rely on DNA-based lentiviral transduction, which permanently alters the T-cell genome. Crucially, traditional DNA CAR-T requires patients to undergo intense, highly toxic preconditioning lymphodepleting chemotherapy (usually a fludarabine and cyclophosphamide regimen) to prevent host rejection of the engineered cells and create immunological space.

Descartes-08 brilliantly bypasses this toxicity by utilizing transient messenger RNA (mRNA) engineering. By using mRNA to express the chimeric antigen receptor targeting the B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA), the CAR expression on the T-cells is temporary. This transient profile entirely eliminates the need for lymphodepleting preconditioning chemotherapy, allowing Descartes-08 to be administered safely in a standard outpatient clinical setting across a brief, six-week course of once-weekly infusions.

Despite its transient engineering, Descartes-08 delivers remarkable, sustained clinical results. Data from the phase 2b randomized, placebo-controlled trial demonstrated that outpatient Descartes-08 administration was associated with clinically meaningful improvements in both MGC and MG-ADL scores in AChR-positive patients, significantly outperforming placebo. At three months, 66.7% of treated patients achieved an MGC response compared to 27.3% in the placebo group, with therapeutic benefits and group differences persisting up to 12 months post-infusion. Safety signals were remarkably benign, largely restricted to mild, transient infusion-related reactions that predictably resolved within 48 hours. Capitalizing on these strong efficacy signals, Cartesian Therapeutics is actively enrolling approximately 100 patients into the pivotal phase 3 AURORA trial throughout 2026, aiming to solidify mRNA CAR-T as a safe, first-in-class cellular therapy for MG. The platform is also expanding into other indications, with the TRITON trial for myositis and the HELIOS trial for juvenile dermatomyositis underway.
5.2 KYV-101: DNA-Based CAR-T and the Pursuit of Complete Remission
This subsection details the high-potency approach of KYV-101. It covers the stunning clinical benchmarks achieved in refractory patients, including complete freedom from traditional immunosuppressive therapies.

Taking a distinct but equally compelling approach, Kyverna Therapeutics is advancing KYV-101, a fully human anti-CD19 CAR-T cell therapy. Unlike Descartes-08, KYV-101 utilizes traditional DNA-based engineering, thereby necessitating a standard lymphodepletion regimen prior to the single-dose infusion. However, the permanence of the DNA integration facilitates a profound, long-lasting eradication of CD19-positive B-cells deep within lymphoid tissues and potentially non-lymphoid reservoirs.

Interim data from the phase 2 segment of the registrational KYSA-6 clinical trial, presented extensively at late 2025 and early 2026 medical congresses (including the MGFA Scientific Session and an oral presentation at AAN on April 20, 2026), have established stunning new clinical benchmarks. In a cohort of severe, refractory gMG patients, a single infusion of KYV-101 yielded a 100% response rate, defined as clinically meaningful reductions (>= 3 points) in both MG-ADL and QMG scores. At 24 weeks post-infusion, patients demonstrated extraordinary mean reductions of –8.0 points on the MG-ADL and –7.7 points on the QMG scales.

Most remarkably, the deep tissue B-cell depletion appears to facilitate a holistic immune system reset. The therapy has been shown to have a positive impact on a broader set of immune cell types, allowing regulatory T-cells—which are essential for keeping the immune system in check—to re-establish immunological tolerance. As a result, 100% of the extensively tracked patients achieved complete freedom from all background non-steroidal immunosuppressive therapies (NSISTs), high-dose corticosteroids (>10mg), and previously required biological agents (FcRn and complement inhibitors) for up to 24 weeks. Furthermore, two-thirds of the patients evaluated beyond the 24-week threshold achieved Minimal Symptom Expression (MSE), the clinical equivalent of absolute disease remission.

Despite the requirement for prior lymphodepletion chemotherapy, KYV-101 demonstrated a highly manageable safety profile in the MG cohort. Notably, investigators reported zero instances of high-grade cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and no events of immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS)—two severe and potentially fatal adverse events frequently associated with oncological CAR-T applications. Based on these unprecedented outcomes, the phase 3 portion of the KYSA-6 trial is actively recruiting in 2026, with an estimated primary completion date in September 2027.

Other highly specialized cellular therapies, such as MuSK-CAART developed by Cabaletta Bio, are also under clinical development. MuSK-CAART is designed specifically for MuSK-antibody positive patients, functioning as a targeted T-cell therapy that seeks out and destroys only the specific B-cells producing anti-MuSK antibodies, offering an incredibly precise, bespoke intervention.
6.0 Comparative Effectiveness, Meta-Analyses, and Safety Profiles
This section evaluates the relative efficacy and safety of different drug classes using complex statistical models. It addresses the rarity of head-to-head trials and the reliance on indirect comparisons.

As the armamentarium of targeted therapies expands exponentially, clinical decision-making is increasingly reliant on sophisticated comparative effectiveness research. Because head-to-head randomized controlled trials between advanced biologics remain exceptionally rare, researchers rely heavily on complex statistical models, such as Matching-Adjusted Indirect Comparisons (MAIC) and Bayesian Network Meta-Analyses (NMA), to evaluate relative efficacy across different drug classes and guide formulary decisions.
6.1 Efficacy Discrepancies Across Drug Classes
This subsection analyzes the performance differences between FcRn blockers, C5 inhibitors, and B-cell depletion therapies. It notes that while general efficacies are comparable, individual patient biomarkers play a significant role in outcomes.

Recent indirect treatment comparisons have attempted to stratify the efficacy of the newly approved agents. In a comprehensive analysis comparing the three leading FcRn blockers—nipocalimab, efgartigimod, and rozanolixizumab—Bucher ITCs and MAICs indicated that all three drugs share a comparable, rapid onset of action, generating significant MG-ADL reductions by Week 1. However, the analysis highlighted potential sustained superiority for nipocalimab. Adjusting for cross-trial heterogeneity using factors such as time since diagnosis, sex, and baseline MG-ADL scores, nipocalimab demonstrated significantly greater MG-ADL change from baseline versus efgartigimod starting at Week 8 and sustained up to 24 weeks (p < 0.05). It similarly outperformed rozanolixizumab in area-under-the-curve (AUC) measurements, which serve as a marker for cumulative therapeutic effect over time.

Furthermore, a massive Bayesian network meta-analysis of 27 randomized placebo-controlled trials published in late 2025 evaluated nine different treatment categories encompassing 2,318 total patients. The analysis concluded that, on average, patients treated with FcRn inhibitors experienced a slightly deeper reduction in clinician-reported QMG scores (–3.6 points) compared to those receiving C5 complement inhibitors (–2.59 points) and CD19 B-cell depletion therapy (–2.50 points). However, sensitivity analyses noted that differences in underlying trial populations, baseline disease severity, age demographics, and the percentage of female patients strongly confound these treatment effects. This suggests that FcRn inhibitors, C5 inhibitors, and CD19 targeted therapies generally possess broadly comparable overarching efficacies, with specific clinical superiority heavily dependent on individual patient biomarkers, autoantibody serotype, and specific clinical histories.
6.2 Safety and Tolerability Profiles
This subsection outlines the specific safety signatures of each therapeutic class. It highlights risks such as headache and hypogammaglobulinemia for FcRn inhibitors, and meningococcal infection for C5 inhibitors.

Understanding the adverse event (AE) profile of each class is paramount for therapeutic selection. Real-world pharmacovigilance and meta-analysis data reveal distinct, highly specific safety signatures for the different biological modalities.

Patients treated with FcRn inhibitors statistically exhibit higher odds of experiencing treatment-related adverse events compared to placebo (Odds Ratio: 2.20). The most ubiquitous AE associated with the FcRn class is mild-to-moderate headache, frequently occurring during the initial infusion cycles. Additionally, the non-specific clearance of IgG creates a transient, dose-dependent hypogammaglobulinemia, marginally elevating the risk for upper and lower respiratory tract infections. Despite this elevated overall AE incidence, the events are rarely severe enough to necessitate treatment discontinuation.

Conversely, C5 complement inhibitors and CD19 B-cell depleting agents demonstrate overall AE odds that are statistically comparable to placebo and standard-of-care. However, the specific risks associated with complement inhibition are critical to note; by blocking MAC formation, C5 inhibitors completely compromise the innate immune system's primary defense mechanism against encapsulated bacteria. While the general AE rate is low, there is a documented, potentially severe risk for systemic meningococcal infections, strictly requiring prophylactic vaccination protocols prior to the initiation of therapy. A large real-world comparative study of 69 patients by Marini et al. noted a higher trend of myasthenic exacerbations (34.6% vs 16.3%) and crises (15.4% vs 5%) in patient cohorts managed with eculizumab compared to FcRn inhibitors. However, the differences did not reach statistical significance and likely reflect the historical use of C5 inhibitors strictly in the most highly refractory, severely ill patient populations.
6.3 The Ultimate Head-to-Head Trial: EPIC
This subsection introduces the EPIC trial, the first randomized head-to-head study comparing nipocalimab and efgartigimod. It describes the study's architecture and its goal of providing definitive clinical insights.

While statistical modeling provides useful inferences, the neurological field is eagerly awaiting empirical confirmation. Unveiled in late 2025 at the American Association of Neuromuscular & Electrodiagnostic Medicine (AANEM) meeting, the EPIC trial is a truly groundbreaking phase 3 study marking the first-ever randomized, head-to-head clinical trial testing two advanced FDA-approved therapeutics in gMG. The study directly pits nipocalimab against efgartigimod in a cohort of 80 FcRn-naïve adult patients possessing an MG-ADL score of greater than or equal to 5.

Participants are randomized 1:1 to either nipocalimab every 2 weeks for 12 weeks or efgartigimod weekly at 10 mg/kg for 4 weeks. Furthermore, the study architecture includes a sophisticated experimental "switching arm." This arm allows patients previously treated with efgartigimod who experience waning efficacy at the end of their treatment cycles to cross over to a 12-week course of nipocalimab. When completed, the EPIC trial will provide definitive, real-world clinical insights into drug superiority, optimal class switching dynamics, and lifecycle management strategies for the entire FcRn inhibitor class.

7.0 The Clinical Research Ecosystem in 2026
This section highlights the massive infrastructure supporting MG research. It lists major medical centers and innovative trials like CNP-106, as well as the role of patient registries.

The rapid advancement of these therapeutics is heavily reliant on a massive, highly integrated clinical research infrastructure. Major medical centers across the United States are actively recruiting for next-generation trials. For instance, the Mayo Clinic, with its premier campuses in Rochester, Phoenix/Scottsdale, and Jacksonville, is heavily involved in advanced MG clinical trials, offering access to cutting-edge protocols for patients suffering from severe or impending myasthenic crisis.

Similarly, the Barrow Neurological Institute (BNI) in Phoenix, Arizona, remains a central hub for neurological innovation. Barrow's Department of Neurology is currently recruiting patients for highly experimental trials, such as the COUR CNP-106 trial. CNP-106 utilizes a completely novel approach, employing nanoparticles designed to retrain the body's immune system to tolerate MG autoantigens, representing a potential divergence from both immunosuppression and B-cell depletion. Banner Health, also located in Phoenix, is contributing to the ecosystem by participating in the ADAPT-EARLY trial, evaluating the clinical outcomes of subcutaneous efgartigimod specifically in patients with new-onset generalized myasthenia gravis.

Underpinning these clinical trials is the vital data collection facilitated by patient advocacy groups. The Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America (MGFA) heavily promotes the Vitaccess Real MG Registry (VRMG). This prospective international observational patient registry links clinical medical records with patient-reported data captured via a digital platform. By tracking the longitudinal impact of MG on daily living, fatigue, and health-related quality of life, the registry provides researchers with the invaluable real-world evidence required to contextualize the true impact of the biological therapies discussed throughout this report.
8.0 Conclusions and Future Directions
This section summarizes the shift toward precision immunomodulation. It looks forward to the potential of cellular therapies to transform MG from a chronic condition into a functionally curable pathology.

The pharmacological management of myasthenia gravis has successfully and irreversibly transitioned from an era of generalized, toxic immunosuppression to an era of precision immunomodulation. The baseline standard of care in 2026 dictates that while conventional agents (pyridostigmine, low-dose daily corticosteroids, early thymectomy) serve as the initial therapeutic foundation, patients demonstrating refractoriness or suffering from unacceptable steroid toxicity must be rapidly transitioned to targeted biological agents.

The selection among these advanced therapies is becoming increasingly algorithm-driven, dictated by specific autoantibody serotypes and patient-specific pathophysiological profiles. For patients requiring rapid, systemic clearance of circulating autoantibodies across diverse serotypes (AChR, MuSK, LRP4), the expanded FcRn inhibitor class—led by continuous-suppression agents like nipocalimab—offers swift and broad-spectrum symptom relief. Conversely, for AChR-positive patients prioritizing immediate preservation of the postsynaptic architecture and innate protection of the neuromuscular junction, terminal complement inhibitors such as zilucoplan and the emerging nanobody gefurulimab provide highly effective, self-administered defense mechanisms.

The recent approval of the CD19-targeting inebilizumab solidifies the critical importance of attacking the source of the pathology—the plasmablast. It yields exceptional durability and validates the possibility of safely maintaining deep disease control with merely two infusions per year while simultaneously eliminating corticosteroid dependence entirely.

Looking immediately forward, the horizon of MG therapeutics is defined by the absolute quest for genuine biological remission. The astonishing phase 2 data emerging from the cellular immunotherapy sector—specifically the outpatient mRNA-based Descartes-08 and the highly potent DNA-based KYV-101 CAR-T platforms—suggests that a total and durable "immune reset" is not just theoretical, but clinically achievable. If the ongoing phase 3 AURORA and KYSA-6 clinical trials successfully replicate the safety and 100% response rates observed in earlier phases, CAR-T cell therapy will fundamentally disrupt the chronic disease management paradigm. It holds the ultimate potential to transform myasthenia gravis from a lifelong, heavily medicated condition into a functionally curable pathology accessible via a single, targeted cellular intervention.
