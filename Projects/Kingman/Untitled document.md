3.0 Overview and Epidemiology
Myasthenia gravis is a rare, chronic autoimmune and neuromuscular disorder. Recent epidemiological data indicates a rising prevalence, particularly an unprecedented increase among older men. The overall prevalence in the United States is estimated at approximately 37 cases per 100,000 individuals. The disease typically exhibits a bimodal distribution: early-onset predominantly affects young adult women under the age of 50, while late-onset predominantly affects older men.
4.0 Pathophysiology and Etiology
The condition is characterized by a failure in nerve-to-muscle communication at the neuromuscular junction. In genetically susceptible individuals, the immune system mistakenly produces autoantibodies that attack specific postsynaptic membrane proteins.

Primary Targets: The most common target is the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (AChR). When these receptors are blocked, altered, or destroyed, muscles cannot contract normally.
Secondary Targets: Less commonly, autoantibodies target other essential proteins such as muscle-specific kinase (MuSK) or low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 4 (LRP4).
Thymic Involvement: The thymus gland plays a critical role in pathogenesis; thymic hyperplasia or tumors (thymomas) are frequently found in patients with the disease, driving the abnormal immune response.
5.0 Clinical Manifestations
The hallmark symptom of myasthenia gravis is fatigable muscle weakness that worsens with physical exertion and improves with rest. Symptoms typically fluctuate throughout the day.

Ocular Weakness: More than 50% of patients first experience eye-related symptoms, including drooping eyelids (ptosis) and double vision (diplopia).
Bulbar Weakness: Involvement of the muscles controlling the face and throat leads to difficulties with chewing, swallowing (dysphagia), speaking (causing slurred or nasal speech), and limited facial expressions.
Generalized Weakness: Weakness often spreads to the limbs, affecting proximal muscles more than distal ones, resulting in difficulty walking, climbing stairs, or lifting the arms.
Myasthenic Crisis: In severe cases, weakness extends to the respiratory muscles, causing life-threatening breathing difficulties that require emergency medical intervention and ventilatory support.
6.0 Diagnostic Testing
Diagnosis relies on a combination of clinical evaluation and specialized testing protocols.

Physical and Neurological Exams: Checking reflexes, muscle strength, and specific bedside tests like the "ice pack test" (applying cold to a droopy eyelid to observe if weakness temporarily resolves).
Serological Testing: Blood tests to detect the presence of AChR, MuSK, or LRP4 autoantibodies. Patients lacking these detectable antibodies are classified as having seronegative myasthenia gravis.
Electrodiagnostics: Repetitive nerve stimulation (RNS) and single-fiber electromyography (SFEMG) are utilized to measure electrical activity and detect impaired nerve-to-muscle signaling.
Imaging: Computed Tomography (CT) or Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) scans of the chest are routinely ordered to check for thymomas or an enlarged thymus gland.
7.0 Current Treatment Options
While there is currently no cure, myasthenia gravis is highly treatable and manageable.

Symptomatic Treatment: Cholinesterase inhibitors (e.g., pyridostigmine) prevent the breakdown of acetylcholine, temporarily improving muscle strength and endurance.
Chronic Immunosuppression: Corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone) and non-steroidal immunosuppressants (e.g., azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, cyclosporine) suppress the abnormal immune response over the long term.
Targeted Therapies: Recent advancements include monoclonal antibodies and targeted biologics. These involve complement inhibitors (eculizumab, ravulizumab, zilucoplan) and neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) inhibitors (efgartigimod, rozanolixizumab) which help reduce harmful autoantibodies or block specific immune cascade pathways.
Rapid Immunomodulation: Plasmapheresis and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) are utilized for severe exacerbations or myasthenic crises to quickly filter out or neutralize circulating autoantibodies.
Surgical Intervention: Thymectomy (surgical removal of the thymus gland) is strictly recommended for patients with a thymoma and is heavily utilized to improve long-term clinical outcomes in many patients without tumors.
