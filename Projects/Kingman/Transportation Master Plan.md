EXECUTIVE SUMM ARY 
I N T R O D U C T I O N 
Kingman is located in Mohave County in northwestern Arizona along Interstate 40 (I-40) and was  incorporated as a city in 1952. Kingman and the surrounding area have experienced sustained  population growth over the last several decades, resulting in a need to provide upgraded transportation  infrastructure. This Transportation Master Plan (TMP) is an update to the Kingman Area  Transportation Study (KATS), which was completed in 2011. This TMP is a strategic document that  will guide transportation decisions the City of Kingman (City) will make, establishing a clear vision for  the future. The process is based on foundational community values and specific policies and  expectations outlined in the 2014 Kingman General Plan Update.  
P L A N N I N G P R O C E S S 
The Kingman TMP planning process is shown in Figure 1 and is composed of three main project  phases: Current and Future Conditions, Alternatives Analysis, and Final Report. The process has a  heavy focus on community and citizen engagement, with opportunities for stakeholder feedback  occurring throughout. It is important to recognize that individuals within Kingman have considerable  insight into the places they live, work, and travel, and are therefore instrumental in the long-term  success of the Kingman TMP.

To address the identified issues and deficiencies, 102 potential solutions were drafted. Potential  solutions were categorized by project types that pertain to different modes of transportation: roadway,  transit, and active transportation. These goals were ranked by the public for roadway,  active transportation, and transit modes of travel during the first round of public engagement. 
Following the prioritization process, solutions were organized into five equally-sized categories based  on their priority score: High, Mid-High, Mid, Mid-Low, and Low. The solution prioritization results are  shown in Figure 4.

Many of the potential solutions overlap one another or address separate issues in the same location  or along the same corridor. To reduce redundancy of improvements to the transportation system, the  potential solutions were combined into a set of projects. Refining the solutions into projects allows the  City to address the needs of Kingman’s transportation system in a manageable and efficient way. The  102 potential solutions were consolidated into 67 transportation projects.  
The total cost of TMP projects is approximately $700 million. As this amount is much larger than the  City will likely have available to invest in the transportation system through 2045, the projects were  stratified into three priority levels (High Priority, Medium Priority, and Low Priority) to indicate what  level of focus the City and associated partner agencies (ADOT and Mohave County) should put into  implementing each project. P U B L I C E N G A G E M E N T  
Public participation and input are key when developing a TMP that responds to Kingman transportation  needs. Two rounds of public engagement were conducted:  
• The first round of public engagement took place during the existing and future conditions  analysis and aimed to gather public opinion on existing conditions, future projections, and  needs and deficiencies. Round 1 of public engagement consisted of an interactive map tool  and virtual survey as well as a presentation to the City Council 
• The second round of public engagement took place while developing transportation projects  and aimed to share the findings of the solution/project development and gather public opinion  on the draft proposed projects. Round 2 of public engagement consisted of an informational video describing updates to date as well as maps and tables of the draft proposed projects.  Round 2 also included a presentation to the City Council 
To notify the public of the engagement efforts, utility bill inserts were mailed to study area residents  during each round. Social media postings were also shared by the City of Kingman on Facebook.

A press release was also issued by the City, notifying the public of the project and its associated  website and engagement efforts. The press release was posted to the City of Kingman’s website and  published by the Kingman Miner newspaper.

O T H E R R E C O M M E N D A T I O N S 
Besides implementing the High Priority projects as funding becomes available, it is recommended that  the City consider the following actions: 
• Update the City’s 2018 Streets and Sidewalks Development Rules and Regulations to include  more guidance related to roadway spacing, access management, median breaks, and signal  spacing 
• Update the City’s Traffic Impact Analysis (TIA) guidelines to reflect post-Covid pandemic trip  generation and parking rates 
• Prepare an Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) Transition Plan that develops  implementation phasing for addressing City facilities and infrastructure in the public right-of way that are not fully compliant with ADA requirements 
• Prepare an Active Transportation Plan that conducts a more detailed analysis of pedestrian  and bicycle infrastructure condition and gaps and recommends ways to better promote safe,  efficient, and comfortable travel for vulnerable road users such as pedestrians and bicyclists. This plan should incorporate local roadways as well as the collector and arterial roadways  studied as part of the TMP 
• Coordinate with ADOT to add Anson Smith Road and Fort Beale Drive as Major Collectors in  the federal functional classification system 
• Develop and adopt through City Council a “Vision Zero” policy statement about the City making  it a priority to provide safe mobility for all with the aspirational goal of ultimately eliminating  fatalities on the transportation system

1. INTRODUCTION 
Kingman is located in Mohave County in northwestern Arizona along Interstate 40 (I-40), just over 100  miles southeast of Las Vegas and approximately 165 miles northwest of Phoenix. Kingman was  incorporated as a city in 1952 and encompasses 35.5 square miles. The 2020 population of Kingman  was 32,689. Kingman and the surrounding area have experienced sustained population growth over  the last several decades. With this growth comes a need to provide upgraded transportation  infrastructure, which will be guided by this comprehensive multimodal Transportation Master Plan  (TMP). This TMP is an update to the Kingman Area Transportation Study (KATS), which was  completed in 2011 and has set the vision for Kingman’s transportation system since that time. 
W H A T I S A T R A N S P O R T A T I O N M A S T E R P L A N ?  
A TMP is a strategic document that guides transportation decisions the City of Kingman (City) will  make with its limited local, state, and federal funding opportunities. The process is based on  foundational community values and specific policies and expectations outlined in the 2014 Kingman  General Plan Update. The TMP sets a clear vision for how investments are made that balance the  city’s rural character with the growing urban mobility needs as well as guides future development  requirements by identifying needed transportation improvements that are consistent with the core  values of the community.

P R E V I O U S P L A N N I N G E F F O R T S  
The Kingman TMP builds on previous planning efforts done by the City, Arizona Department of  Transportation (ADOT), and the Western Arizona Council of Governments (WACOG). The following are short descriptions of the primary plans that have influenced this TMP and support long-term  transportation planning efforts. 
K I N G M A N A R E A T R A N S P O R T A T I O N S T U D Y ( 2 0 1 1 ) 
This plan provided a comprehensive transportation vision for the Kingman  area. The purpose of the KATS was to identify, analyze, and recommend  short-, mid-, and long-range transportation improvements, including recommendations for new roadway connections, roadway widening,  intersection improvements, new freeway interchanges, modifications to  existing interchanges, railroad crossings, functional classification changes,  transit improvements, new and improved on-street bicycle and pedestrian  facilities, and new multi-use pathways. KATS also included planning-level cost  estimates and an implementation plan. 
S T O C K T O N H I L L R O A D C O R R I D O R S T U D Y ( 2 0 1 4 ) 
The Stockton Hill Road Corridor Study assessed the City’s transportation and  development framework needs for the Stockton Hill Road corridor as well as  recommend solutions to improve multimodal mobility and the built  environment. Primary framework and policy recommendations included  setting development standards along the corridor, developing a street network  policy to increase connectivity, and setting a multimodal policy to gradually  increase options for non-motorized travel. Infrastructure improvement  recommendations included traffic signal timing optimization, access  management, intersection improvements at Airway Avenue, and bike lane,  sidewalk, and pedestrian crossing improvements. 
C I T Y O F K I N G M A N G E N E R A L P L A N U P D A T E 2 0 3 0 ( 2 0 1 4 ) 
The General Plan provides a broad overview of current conditions and issues  associated with land use, demographics, the transportation system, public  facilities and services, open space, and recreational facilities in Kingman. The  plan is a key component in setting a long-term vision for land development,  growth management, healthcare services, the natural environment, and  recreation. For the transportation element of the General Plan, the 2011 KATS  was referenced as the guiding vision. In the land use element, several growth  areas were identified. Each of these growth areas included an Access and  Circulation section that outlined how these areas would integrate with the  surrounding transportation system.

K I N G M A N A I R P O R T M A S T E R P L A N ( 2 0 2 1 ) 
The Airport Master Plan provides an in-depth evaluation of Kingman Municipal  Airport’s aviation inventory, demand, and an overview of the systematic  development that will best meet those demands. The master plan recommends a development concept that outlines specific uses for all areas of airport  property, including strategies for revenue enhancement. Recommendations for  landside improvements that impact the surrounding transportation system  included designating locations for additional vehicle parking and new airport  access extending from the south. The plan also included land use  recommendations that may impact adjacent streets. 
K A R T S H O R T - A N D L O N G - R A N G E T R A N S I T P L A N ( 2 0 2 1 ) 
This plan identifies the successful components of the existing Kingman Area  Regional Transit (KART) system, proposes strategies to improve transit  service, and creates a blueprint for addressing the region’s unmet  transportation needs. The plan guides future transit planning, operations,  capital investment, and policy decisions for the KART system. The plan  identifies fiscally constrained improvements over the next five years as well as  long-term improvements aimed at shifting more of the region’s travel to transit  from personal vehicles. The plan also includes a marketing plan to refresh the  brand of KART service and increase awareness. 
C I T Y O F K I N G M A N C A P I T A L I M P R O V E M E N T P L A N ( 2 0 2 3 ) 
The City develops a comprehensive budget and Capital Improvement Plan  (CIP) every year. The current CIP covers Fiscal Year (FY) 2023-2027 and lays  out programmed transportation improvement projects to be constructed.  Estimated costs and anticipated year of expenditure are provided for each  project. The prior year’s CIP also included a list of high-priority transportation  projects that are not currently able to be funded through the CIP. These  projects will need to be funded through external sources such as grants, an  allocation by City Council, or programmed in future CIPs. These future projects  are considered in the TMP. 
W A C O G R E G I O N A L T R A N S I T I M P L E M E N T A T I O N P L A N  ( O N G O I N G ) 
This plan assesses the demand and feasibility of a region-wide  
transit service that connects unincorporated areas of Mohave  
and La Paz Counties to urbanized cities, including Kingman,  
Bullhead City, and Lake Havasu City. The plan is currently in  
progress and will aim to identify opportunities to better serve  
intercity transit needs, as well as define practical alternatives  
for regional transit service.

R A N C H O S A N T A F E T I 
This project will construct a new interchange on I-40 west of Rattlesnake  Wash, along the Mohave Drive Section Line alignment, with eventual arterial  street connections to Hualapai Mountain Road, Airway Avenue, and the  Airport Industrial Park. The project is comprised of two phases. The first phase  is programmed for construction starting in 2022 and includes the TI on I-40  and a new arterial roadway (Rancho Santa Fe Parkway) between Louise  Avenue and Industrial Boulevard. The first phase also includes an extension  of Airway Avenue from its current terminus to Rancho Santa Fe Parkway. A  future second phase will extend the arterial roadway south from Louise  Avenue to Hualapai Mountain Road. Funding for the second phase has not  yet been identified and there is no timeline for construction. 
K I N G M A N C R O S S I N G T I 
This project improves access to the area surrounding the Kingman Regional  Medical Center Hualapai Mountain Campus and includes a variety of roadway  improvements in addition to the new TI on I-40. The initial phase of the project,  which includes the TI, auxiliary lanes on I-40, and a new arterial roadway  connection between I-40 and Santa Rosa Drive, has been funded for  construction in 2022. Future phases of the project are not currently funded  but include extensions of Kingman Crossing north from Santa Rosa Drive to  Airway Avenue at Lyons Road and south from the TI to Louise Avenue east  of Sage Street. Additional extensions of Airfield Avenue and Santa Rosa Drive  are also planned to provide additional east-west connectivity and increase  access to the new TI.

S O C I O E C O N O M I C A N A L Y S I S 
Socioeconomic analysis in transportation planning is an examination of how a proposed improvement  will impact the overall social and economic character of an area and the well-being of current and  future residents. Socioeconomic conditions were analyzed to identify traditionally underserved  populations for purposes of equity analysis. The assessed underserved populations within the study  area include:  
• Persons with Limited English Proficiency (LEP). Identification of LEP persons can be  informative for the purpose of devising appropriate strategies for meaningful public  involvement and ensuring LEP persons are able to weigh in on transportation decisions. LEP  persons comprise approximately 1.3% of the study area population 
• Minority Populations. ADOT and the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) define five  minority groups, as follows: 
• Black (a person having origins in any of the black racial groups of Africa)  • Hispanic or Latino (a person of Mexican, Puerto Rican, Cuban, Central or South American,  or other Spanish culture or origin, regardless of race)  
• Asian American (a person having origins in any of the original people of the Far East,  Southeast Asia, or the Indian subcontinent)  
• American Indian and Alaskan Native (a person having origins in any of the original people  of North America, South America, including Central America, and who maintains cultural  identification through tribal affiliation or community recognition)  
• Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander (people having origins in any of the original  people of Hawaii, Guam, Samoa, or other Pacific Islands)  
Minority populations comprise approximately 29% of the study area population, with Hispanics accounting for most of the study area’s minority population 
• Low-Income Population. Low-income populations are those whose median annual  household income is at or below the Department of Health and Human Services poverty  guidelines for a family of four, which is currently $27,750. Approximately 12% of households  in the study area are low-income households 
• Internet Availability. The ability to have reliable internet connectivity is a challenge in rural  areas throughout Arizona and the United States. Approximately 17% of residents in the study  area do not have access to reliable internet 
• Zero-Vehicle Households. Households without access to a personal vehicle can exist for  several reasons, including residents having a disability, preferring not to drive, or are not being  able to afford the expense of owning a vehicle. These households are reliant on alternative  methods of transportation, such as transit, active transportation modes, or rides from other  community members. Approximately 5.4% of the study area population does not have access  to a personal vehicle

FU N C T I O N A L C L A S S I F I C A T I O N 
Roadways are classified based on the type of traffic they are  intended to serve. For example, arterials move people for long  distances at higher speeds within a city or between cities.  Collector streets are lower speed and shorter distances than  arterials and connect travelers to the arterials. Local streets are  very low speed, extend for short distances, and provide direct  access to residential and commercial properties. This  categorization is referred to as functional classification. Functional classifications have an inverse relationship between  access and mobility, as shown in Figure 22, based on the types  of trips they are intended to serve. 
Figure 22. Mobility/Access  Relationship
There are instances where a roadway’s function may not match its infrastructure. Examples could  include overbuilding a road to accommodate future growth or unanticipated or unplanned growth  causing capacity issues on older roadways. Identifying these potential mismatches is important to  ensure there is adequate planning to appropriately size existing and future roadways to avoid negative  consequences that could include cut-through traffic, pavement degradation, traffic congestion,  decreased safety, or overinvestment in roadways that will not need a high capacity. 
F E D E R A L F U N C T I O N A L C L A S S I F I C A T I O N  
Three main functional classes are defined by FHWA:  
arterial, collector, and local. These classifications are  based on speed, vehicular capacity, and relationships  with adjacent land uses according to the character of  service they are intended to provide (Table 6). ADOT  recently collaborated with jurisdictions to update the  federal functional classifications statewide. The federal  functional classification map for the study area is  shown in Figure 23. This map reflects the recently  updated functional classifications ADOT has assigned. 
Why is Federal Functional  Classification Important? 
A roadway must be federally  functionally classified as a Collector,  Arterial, or Interstate to be eligible for  federal transportation funding for  improvements.

C I T Y F U N C T I O N A L C L A S S I F I C A T I O N 
In addition to the federal functional classifications defined by FHWA and dictated by ADOT, the City  of Kingman has its own set of functional classifications that are used for planning and programming  purposes (see Streets and Sidewalks Development Rules and Regulations (2018)). These  classifications are largely only for roads within Kingman’s boundaries and are used to highlight  corridors of importance at the local level. The classifications are similar to FHWA’s and are shown in  Figure 24: 
• Major Arterial. A continuous street tying together two or more traffic generating areas used  primarily for through movement of traffic between separate areas 
• Minor Arterial. A principal traffic artery tying together residential and commercial areas that  conveys traffic from Major Arterials to lower-order streets 
• Collector. A connecting street between two arterial streets that provides access to local  streets 
• Local. Provides access to properties and conducts traffic between dwelling units and higher order streets 
Many of the roadways have a similar level of classification between the federal and City of Kingman  designations. Notable differences include: 
• Andy Devine Avenue is a Major Arterial for the entire length north of I-40 whereas some  portions are classified as a Minor Arterial in the federal classifications 
• Stockton Hill Road, Hualapai Mountain Road, Gordon Drive, Airway Avenue, and Bank Street  are all classified as Major Arterials by the City of Kingman but are federally classified as Minor  Arterials or Major Collectors 
• Several Collectors classified by the City are different from those federally classified
